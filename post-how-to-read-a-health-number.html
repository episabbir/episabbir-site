How to read a health number in the news

By Md Sabbir Ahmed · November 5, 2026 · About 2 minutes

Health news is full of numbers. A number feels exact, but it only tells part of the story. Ask these four questions.

1. Out of how many?

Town A had 50 flu cases. Town B had 10. Town A sounds worse. But Town A has 10,000 people and Town B has only 500. That is 1 in 200 people in Town A, and 1 in 50 in Town B. So Town B had the bigger problem. (These towns are made up.)

Comparing a number to the number of people who live there is called a rate. It is the fair way to compare places.

2. Double of what?

If a risk goes from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000, a headline can say "Risk doubles!" That is true. But 9,998 out of 10,000 people still do not get sick. Always look for the real numbers behind "double."

This really happened. In 1995, the United Kingdom warned that some birth control pills doubled the chance of a blood clot. Many women stopped taking the pill, even though the real chance of a clot was very small. The next year, England and Wales had about 13,600 more abortions.

3. Link or cause?

In summer, people eat more ice cream. They also get more sunburns. Ice cream does not cause sunburn. Sunny weather causes both. When the news says something is "linked to" an illness, that usually means a link, not proof.

4. Who counted, and when?

Trust numbers from health agencies and checked studies more than numbers from a random post online. Also check the date. Health numbers are updated often. Two stories can show different numbers just because they were written on different days.

Next time you see a health number, ask: Out of how many? Double of what? Link or cause? Who counted, and when?
